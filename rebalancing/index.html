Rebalancing: When to Act and When to Leave It

Rebalancing too often is as harmful as never rebalancing. The right trigger is drift beyond a threshold, not a calendar date. For most investors, once a year is enough.

What rebalancing is and why it matters

Rebalancing means restoring your portfolio to its target allocation after market movements have caused it to drift. If you targeted 70% equity and a strong equity year has pushed you to 80%, rebalancing means selling some equity and buying debt to bring the split back to 70/30.

Without rebalancing, a strong equity market will gradually push your portfolio to higher and higher equity weights. By the time a correction comes, you are more exposed than you intended to be, and the fall hurts more than your plan assumed.

Rebalancing also enforces a buy-low-sell-high discipline systematically. When equity rises above target, you trim it (selling high). When it falls below target, you add to it (buying low). Over long periods, this mechanical discipline adds to returns without requiring any market timing skill.

The cost of over-rebalancing

Rebalancing is not free. Every time you sell equity mutual fund units that have been held less than a year, you pay STCG tax at 20%. For units held longer, LTCG at 12.5% applies above ₹1.25 lakhs. There are also exit loads on recent purchases in most funds.

An investor who rebalances every quarter is generating tax events four times a year. The tax drag over a decade can easily exceed the benefit of maintaining precise allocation. Monthly rebalancing has been shown in multiple studies to produce worse outcomes than annual rebalancing, once costs are accounted for.

Calendar-based rebalancing (every quarter, every month) is almost always too frequent. Threshold-based rebalancing (act when allocation drifts beyond a set amount) is more efficient and usually less frequent.

The 10% drift rule

A practical and widely used threshold: rebalance when the equity percentage drifts more than 10 percentage points from the target.

Target equityRebalance when equity reaches
70%Below 60% or above 80%
60%Below 50% or above 70%
50%Below 40% or above 60%

In most years, a 10% drift threshold means you do not rebalance at all. In a strong bull market, you might trim equity once. In a sharp correction, you might add equity once. The combination of low frequency and mechanical discipline produces good outcomes without excessive tax friction.

How to rebalance without selling

The cheapest rebalancing method creates no tax event at all: redirect new SIP contributions.

If equity has drifted above target, stop new equity SIP and redirect those contributions to debt for a period. If equity has drifted below target, increase equity SIP and pause debt contributions. Over several months, the allocation moves back toward target without any redemptions, no tax, and no exit loads.

This approach only works if the drift is modest and you have sufficient monthly SIP. For large drift after a multi-year bull market, some actual switching may be necessary to bring allocation back in a reasonable timeframe.

Rebalancing at the annual review

Combining rebalancing with the April annual review is the most efficient approach. Once a year you check whether any goal's allocation has drifted beyond the threshold. If it has, you redirect the next few months of SIP to bring it back. If it has not, you do nothing.

For most investors in most years, the annual review will confirm that no rebalancing is required. The portfolio grew, all allocations stayed within band, and the only action needed is a SIP step-up. That is a good outcome. It means the portfolio is doing its job without requiring active management.
