*You probably have more funds than you need. Here is how to know when you have had enough — without any complicated maths.*

---

## The Story of Every Indian Investor's Portfolio

It usually starts with one fund. Then a friend mentions another one. Then a YouTube video calls something a hidden gem. Then the market corrects and someone says "you need international exposure." Then a financial influencer says "flexi cap is the future." Then you open a new app and it recommends five more.

Before you know it, you have twelve funds. And here is the uncomfortable question nobody asks: are those twelve funds actually protecting you better than three good ones would?

The answer, almost always, is no.

More funds does not mean more diversification. After a point, it means the same exposure with more paperwork, more tax complexity, and more chances to make an emotional decision when one of them underperforms.

The question worth asking is not "which fund should I add next?" It is "do I actually need another fund at all?"

---

## What Diversification Is Actually Supposed to Do

Imagine you own two shops. One sells ice cream. One sells umbrellas. On sunny days, ice cream sells well and umbrellas do not. On rainy days, umbrellas sell well and ice cream does not. Either way, one of your shops is doing well. Your total income is steadier than if you owned two ice cream shops.

That is diversification. Two things that do not rise and fall together.

Now imagine you own twelve shops — but all of them sell ice cream. You have twelve shops but you are exposed to the same single risk: bad weather. That is not diversification. That is concentration wearing the costume of diversification.

This is exactly what happens when Indian investors hold eight or twelve mutual funds — because most diversified equity funds in India are all buying the same top 30 large cap stocks. HDFC Bank, Reliance, Infosys, ICICI Bank, TCS — they appear at the top of almost every fund's portfolio.

So when markets fall, all your funds fall together. You feel spread out but you are concentrated.

---

## The Simple Test: Do Your Funds Move Together?

Here is the clearest way to check whether two funds are genuinely diversifying each other.

Look at their returns over the last three years — month by month. In months when Fund A went up, did Fund B also go up? In months when Fund A fell, did Fund B also fall?

If yes — both funds are essentially giving you the same thing. Owning both is like owning the same ice cream shop twice.

If no — the funds are genuinely independent. In bad months for one, the other held up. That is real diversification.

The fancy word for this is correlation. Two funds that always move together have high correlation. Two funds that move independently have low correlation.

You want low correlation between your funds. High correlation means you are paying for the impression of diversification without actually getting it.

---

## The Crucial Test: What Happens During a Crash?

Here is something most investors never check. Two funds might seem different during normal markets — one slightly up, one slightly down, moving independently. But when the market really crashes — down 20%, 30%, 40% — do they fall together?

This matters enormously. Diversification is supposed to protect you when things go badly. If all your funds fall together during a crash, they have failed their one important job.

Two funds that look different in good times but crash together in bad times are providing you with **false diversification**. You feel protected. You are not.

The opposite — two funds that are somewhat similar in normal times but diverge during a crash, where one holds up while the other falls — that is genuine protection.

So do not just check whether your funds move differently on average. Check whether they move differently when markets are falling. That is when it counts.

## Why Each Category Needs Only One Fund

Let us make this concrete. Here is the maximum number of funds you need per category, and the reason:

**Large cap or index funds: one only**

All Nifty 50 index funds track exactly the same index. Two Nifty 50 funds from different AMCs give you identical returns at twice the complexity. If you hold one large cap index fund, you already own a basket of India's biggest 50 companies. A second one does not add a single new company.

**Flexi cap funds: one only**

Flexi cap funds invest across company sizes. But in practice, their largest holdings are the same top 20–30 large cap stocks that dominate every diversified fund. Two flexi cap funds from different houses look different on paper but move almost identically. One is enough.

**Mid cap funds: one only**

The mid cap universe in India — companies ranked 101 to 250 by size — has a limited number of quality stocks. Two active mid cap funds end up owning many of the same companies. One is sufficient.

**Small cap funds: zero or one**

This category requires caution. When small cap stocks crash, they can fall 65–70% and take 7–9 years to recover. For most investors with a goal under 12 years away, small cap funds are simply inappropriate — not because they are bad funds but because the recovery time is too long for the remaining window. If you are beyond 12 years from your goal, one small cap fund at most is appropriate.

**Equity hybrid or multi-asset funds: one only**

These funds already hold equity, debt, and sometimes gold internally. They are built-in diversification. Holding two of them means you have two managers making the same three-way split — adding no new exposure, just more complexity.

**Debt funds (gilt, target maturity): one per goal date**

Debt funds are about matching your goal's date and protecting what you have built. One gilt fund or target maturity fund matched to your goal date is sufficient. Two debt funds of the same type solve no problem.

**Liquid fund: one, sometimes two**

One is sufficient for most people. The only reason to hold a second is if you want to spread across two AMCs for extra safety. Beyond two, you are over-engineering.

## The Ceiling: How Many Is Too Many?

For any single goal — retirement, child education, house purchase — the maximum useful number of funds is **four to five**.

For your entire portfolio across all goals — **seven to ten funds total**.

This sounds restrictive. It is not once you realise something important: the same fund can serve multiple goals. The Nifty 50 index fund you hold for retirement can also be part of your child education allocation. You do not need a separate fund for each goal. You need a separate allocation strategy for each goal, pointing to some of the same underlying funds.

The investor with seven well-chosen funds and clear allocations is better off than the investor with fifteen funds, most of which are quietly doing the same thing.

---

## Seven Signs You Have Too Many Funds

**1. You cannot explain why you hold each fund in one sentence.**

Not what it returned. Not what category it is. Why you specifically hold it — what it does that your other funds do not. If you cannot answer this for every fund, some of them are clutter.

**2. You have two or more funds in the same category.**

Two flexi cap funds. Two mid cap funds. Two large cap funds. There is almost no situation where the second one adds something the first does not.

**3. One fund is less than 5% of your total investment for that goal.**

A fund so small that even if it doubled, it would barely move your total portfolio — that fund is not doing real work. It is there for a reason that made sense once and has not been examined since.

**4. You added a fund in the last year without checking how it moves relative to your existing funds.**

This is how portfolios accumulate. Every new recommendation feels different. Usually it is not.

**5. Your portfolio review takes more than an hour.**

A four-fund portfolio for one goal takes thirty minutes to review properly once a year. If your review takes much longer, the complexity is no longer serving you — it is creating more opportunity for error.

**6. You have never sold a single fund.**

Investing is not just buying. Some funds complete their role and should be exited — either because the timeline changed, because a better option now exists, or because the fund itself changed character. A portfolio where nothing has ever been exited is a portfolio that has never been truly managed.

**7. When one fund underperforms, you panic.**

If you have twelve funds and one of them has a bad year, the temptation to switch is enormous. But with a smaller, well-understood portfolio, you know why each fund is there. A bad year does not shake that understanding. Over-diversification creates over-monitoring which creates over-reacting.

## The One Question That Settles It

Before adding any new fund to your portfolio, ask this one question:

**What does this fund do that none of my existing funds do?**

Not "it returned 18% last year" — your existing fund might have returned 16% and that 2% difference will not be there every year.

Not "it is a different fund house" — different house, same stocks, same outcome.

Not "someone recommended it" — recommendations are not evidence of uniqueness.

The answer must be specific: it accesses a different set of companies (genuinely different category), or it behaves differently during market downturns (proved by looking at how it has moved historically versus your existing funds), or it fills a category you genuinely do not have.

If you cannot answer this specifically, you do not need the fund.

---

## What "Enough" Actually Looks Like

A typical Indian investor with two goals — retirement 20 years away and a child's education 12 years away — genuinely needs only five instruments:

**One Nifty 50 index fund.** The bedrock. Lowest cost, tracks the market, no manager risk. Allocate a portion to both goals.

**One mid cap fund or Nifty Next 50 index fund.** Genuinely different companies from the large cap universe. Real diversification. Only for the retirement goal given the longer timeline.

**One multi-asset fund.** For the child education goal, where you want equity growth but also some stability from gold and debt built in. One fund doing three jobs.

**One or two target maturity gilt funds.** One matched to the education goal date, one matched to the retirement date. Pure debt, government-backed, predictable.

**One liquid fund.** Emergency buffer and parking space.

Five instruments. Two goals. Twenty years of runway. Clean, reviewable, manageable.

Every fund you hold beyond this should justify itself with a specific answer to the one question above. Most cannot. Which means most investors' tenth and twelfth funds are not making them safer. They are making them busier — and busy does not mean better invested.

---

## The Bottom Line

Diversification is not about how many funds you own. It is about whether your funds genuinely behave differently from each other — especially when markets fall.

Three funds that move independently protect you better than twelve funds that all crash together.

The investor who owns four well-chosen funds and understands exactly why each is there will make better decisions, pay less in taxes, spend less time monitoring, and arrive at their goal with less anxiety than the investor who owns fifteen funds they inherited from fifteen separate "this one is better" moments.

You do not need more funds. You need better reasons for the ones you have.
