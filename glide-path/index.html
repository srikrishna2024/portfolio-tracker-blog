The Glide Path: How Your Allocation Should Change as Goals Approach

Most investors set an allocation once and hold it forever. The glide path is the systematic shift from equity to debt as each goal gets closer. Skipping it is one of the most common and costly mistakes in long-term investing.

Why allocation cannot stay constant

Equity is the right asset for long-horizon goals because it provides the growth needed to beat inflation over decades. Equity is the wrong asset for near-term goals because it can fall 30 to 40% in a correction and may not recover before you need the money.

These two facts together mean that the allocation which is correct for a goal 20 years away is incorrect for the same goal when it is 2 years away. The allocation must change as time passes. This gradual shift is the glide path.

An investor who starts with 80% equity for a retirement goal at age 35 and still holds 80% equity at age 58 has not managed their allocation. They have drifted into a risk profile that no longer matches their situation.

How the glide path works

As each year passes and a goal gets one year closer, the recommended equity allocation reduces. The shift happens gradually so there are no large lump-sum moves that could trigger tax or be timed badly.

1

Reduce new equity SIP into that goal

As allocation drifts above target, stop or reduce new SIP into the equity funds serving that goal. New contributions go to debt instead. This is the gentlest form of rebalancing and creates no tax event.

2

Redirect equity growth

When equity funds in a goal grow above the target weight, the next year's SIP goes to debt until the allocation is back on target. Over time this naturally rebalances without selling.

3

Active switch as goal nears 3 to 5 years

When a goal is 3 to 5 years away, the stakes are high enough to switch equity corpus to debt even if it means paying LTCG. A 12.5% tax on gains is far cheaper than a 30% market correction with no recovery time.

Sequence of returns risk: why the last few years matter most

The glide path is not just about reducing volatility. It is about protecting against sequence of returns risk, one of the most underappreciated risks in long-term investing.

Two investors both earn 11% CAGR over 25 years but in different sequences. One has strong early returns and weak late returns. The other has weak early returns and strong late returns. If both are accumulating through the whole period, they end at the same place.

But if the first investor hits a 35% market fall in years 23 to 25, just as they planned to deploy the corpus for retirement, the outcome is permanently damaged. The second investor, who had the bad years early while still accumulating, is unaffected.

The glide path is the practical defence against this risk. By shifting to debt in the final 5 years of a goal, you remove the corpus from equity risk at precisely the period when a bad sequence would cause irreversible harm.

The glide path for retirement vs other goals

Retirement has a unique glide path challenge: the goal is not a single event but a 25 to 30 year withdrawal phase. The corpus needs to last decades after the final accumulation date.

This means the glide path does not end at retirement. In early retirement, a higher equity allocation is still appropriate because the corpus needs to grow to fund decades of withdrawals. The shift to conservative allocation happens gradually through retirement itself, not all at once at age 60.

For other goals (education, house purchase), the glide path does end at the goal date. The full corpus needs to be available on a specific date in relatively stable assets.

Why most investors skip the glide path

Without a goal-based framework, there is no natural trigger to review allocation. The investor who set 80% equity in 2010 still holds 80% equity in 2030 not because they made a conscious decision to maintain it, but because no system prompted them to change it.
