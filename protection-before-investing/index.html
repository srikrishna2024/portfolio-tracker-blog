Before You Invest: Term Insurance, Health Insurance, and an Emergency Fund

There is a step that comes before asset allocation, before fund selection, before SIP optimisation. Without it, a single bad event can undo years of disciplined investing in weeks. Most people skip it because it is boring. It is not optional.

Investing is about building wealth. Protection is about making sure one event does not destroy everything you have built. They are not competing priorities — protection is the foundation that makes long-term investing possible at all.

This post covers three things: term life insurance, health insurance, and an emergency fund. None of them generate returns. All of them are prerequisites to serious long-term investing. Without all three in place, your investment portfolio is built on sand.

Term insurance — the one that matters when you are gone

Term insurance pays a fixed sum to your family if you die during the policy period. That is all it does. That is all it needs to do.

The people who depend on your income — spouse, children, ageing parents — need a financial bridge if you are no longer there to provide it. Without adequate term insurance, a sudden death does not just create grief. It creates an immediate financial crisis: mortgage payments that cannot be made, school fees that must stop, retirements that collapse.

How much cover do you need?

A practical starting point: 10 to 15 times your annual income. The logic is that the payout, if invested at a conservative 6–7%, should replace your income for your dependents until they are financially independent. Adjust upwards if you have large outstanding loans (home loan, car loan), young children who need many years of support, or parents who depend on you.

Example: if your annual income is ₹12 lakh and you have a ₹40 lakh home loan, a ₹1.5–2 crore cover is reasonable. The payout covers the loan and provides an invested corpus to generate ongoing income for your family.

Term only — no riders, no return of premium

A plain term policy is the right choice. Here is why the common alternatives are not:

✗

Return of premium (TROP) — do not buy it

Return-of-premium policies promise to refund the premiums you paid if you survive the policy term. This sounds appealing. The mathematics does not support it. TROP premiums are significantly higher than plain term premiums. The extra amount you pay for "premium return" is money that would have compounded into far more had you invested it yourself. The "returned" amount is also not inflation-adjusted — ₹5 lakh returned in 30 years is worth a fraction of what it is today. Buy the cheapest plain term policy for the cover you need. Invest the premium difference.

✗

Endowment, money-back, and whole-life policies — not recommended

These combine insurance with an investment or savings component. The result is that they do neither well. The insurance cover is typically far lower than what a dedicated term policy would provide for the same premium. The investment returns are typically lower than what pure market investments would generate. The premium is locked for decades. These products exist because they are profitable for insurers and distributors — not because they serve the buyer's interests better than a term policy plus separate investments.

✓

What to buy instead

A plain term policy with adequate cover for the period during which your family depends on your income. The policy term should extend to approximately age 60 or until your planned retirement, whichever is later — by then, your investments should be large enough that your family is no longer dependent on your income.

A critical illness rider (separate add-on, not bundled) that pays a lump sum on diagnosis of cancer, heart attack, stroke, or kidney failure is worth considering. A medical diagnosis that does not kill you but prevents you from working is not covered by death-benefit term insurance, and the treatment costs and income loss are both severe.

When you no longer need term insurance

Term insurance is needed as long as others depend on your income. Once your children are financially independent, your home loan is repaid, and your investment corpus is large enough to support your family without your earned income, the need for term insurance diminishes. Most people in their 50s or early 60s who have accumulated well find that the need has reduced significantly. Do not renew beyond the point of need — the premium is better invested.

The one decision that cannot be undone

Term insurance is underwritten at the time of application. Serious illness, a complex medical history, or advancing age all make it harder or more expensive to get adequate cover later. Buy term insurance while you are young and healthy — before you need to think carefully about whether you can still get it. A 28-year-old pays a fraction of what a 45-year-old pays for the same cover. Delaying is expensive in a way that cannot be recovered.

Health insurance — the one that matters while you are alive

A single medical event — a cardiac bypass, a cancer diagnosis, a serious accident — can cost ₹10–30 lakh or more in out-of-pocket expenses even at a reputable private hospital. Without health insurance, these costs are paid from savings, investments, or borrowed money. They arrive without warning. They cannot be planned for. And they happen to the healthiest people.

Health insurance does not prevent illness. It prevents illness from becoming a financial catastrophe on top of a medical one.

How much cover is enough?

Coverage requirements have increased significantly over the last decade as medical inflation consistently outpaces general inflation. As a guide for metro and tier-1 cities:

Individual in 20s–30s₹10–15 lakhTop-up if employer provides base cover

Individual or couple, 40s–50s₹25–50 lakhMedical costs rise sharply with age

These are approximate. The right number depends on the hospitals you want access to, the city you live in, and your family medical history. Higher is better if affordable — the premium difference between ₹10 lakh and ₹20 lakh cover is often modest relative to the gap in protection.

Employer cover is not enough on its own

Most salaried employees have group health insurance through their employer. This is valuable but insufficient as your only cover for three reasons:

  • It ends when employment ends. Job loss, resignation, or retirement removes the cover at exactly the point when you may have pre-existing conditions that make new insurance difficult or expensive to obtain. Someone who relied on employer cover for 20 years and then loses their job at 52 may find individual cover impossible to get at any reasonable cost.
  • It often covers only hospitalisation. Day procedures, specialist consultations, and pre-hospitalisation costs are frequently excluded.
  • The sum insured is often low. Many employer covers are ₹3–5 lakh — adequate for minor hospitalisation, inadequate for serious illness.

The right approach: maintain an individual or family policy independent of employment. Use employer cover as a supplement, not as your primary protection.

Super top-up policies — a cost-effective way to increase cover

A super top-up policy activates after a deductible threshold (typically ₹5–10 lakh) has been crossed in a policy year. Below the deductible, your base policy pays. Above it, the super top-up takes over. The premium for a ₹50 lakh super top-up with a ₹10 lakh deductible is a fraction of what a standalone ₹50 lakh base policy would cost.

If you have a base policy of ₹10 lakh and a super top-up of ₹40 lakh above ₹10 lakh deductible, a ₹35 lakh hospitalisation bill is covered entirely. This is typically the most cost-efficient structure for adequate protection.

Pre-existing conditions and waiting periods

Health insurance has waiting periods for pre-existing conditions — typically 2 to 4 years before claims related to a known condition are covered. This is the most important reason to buy health insurance young and healthy, before any conditions exist. A 30-year-old buying health insurance with no conditions has no exclusions. A 48-year-old with hypertension and pre-diabetes may face permanent exclusions or may not get adequate cover at all.

Waiting periods also apply to specific conditions (maternity, joint replacement, cataracts) regardless of pre-existing conditions. Read the policy document — not the sales brochure — before buying.

Insure your parents separately and early

Parents over 60 often cannot be added to a family floater policy, or only at very high premiums. Individual policies for parents become harder to obtain and more expensive with every passing year. If your parents do not have independent health insurance, arranging it for them is a financially urgent task — not a nice-to-have. One hospitalisation for an uninsured parent can cost more than several years of their premiums would have. This is also true if you plan to support elderly parents financially — a medical event without cover transfers the full cost to you.

Emergency fund — the buffer that protects everything else

An emergency fund is money that is available immediately, without selling investments, when something goes wrong. It is not an investment. It does not need to generate high returns. Its only job is to be there.

Without an emergency fund, every financial disruption becomes an investment decision made under pressure. Job loss at 38? Sell the equity SIPs. Unexpected car repair? Redeem from the goal fund. Medical expense the insurance did not cover? Break the FD. Each of these is a small destruction of long-term wealth, made not by choice but by necessity.

How much do you need?

3

3 months — minimum for most salaried employees

A salaried employee with a stable job, employer health cover, and no dependents can operate with 3 months of expenses as an emergency buffer. This covers an unexpected job transition, a short medical episode, or a large unplanned bill without touching investments.

6

6 months — standard recommendation for most households

Most working families with a mortgage, children, and one primary income earner need 6 months. A job loss for the primary earner can take 3–6 months to resolve. The buffer must be large enough to cover the full gap without any investment liquidation.

12

12 months — for self-employed, freelancers, and single income households

If your income is irregular or could stop completely — freelancers, business owners, single earners with dependents — a 12-month buffer is the right target. Income disruption for the self-employed can last longer and is harder to predict. The buffer should reflect the realistic worst case for your specific income type.

Where to keep it

The emergency fund requirements, in order of priority: immediately accessible, principal-protected, and earns something above zero. Returns are a distant third concern.

A practical structure for a ₹6-lakh emergency fund: ₹1 lakh in savings account (immediate access), ₹3 lakh in sweep-in FD linked to savings (automatic, earns FD rates), ₹2 lakh in liquid fund (same-day or next-day access). This combination provides full access without any investment liquidation risk.

What the emergency fund is not for

The emergency fund exists for genuine emergencies — unexpected income disruption, urgent medical expense not covered by insurance, critical home or vehicle repair. It is not for:

  • Planned large purchases (holiday, appliance, wedding contribution) — save separately for these
  • Investment opportunities ("I'll use the emergency fund and rebuild it after the IPO") — this thinking has destroyed many emergency funds permanently
  • Paying EMIs that have become unaffordable — if your EMIs require emergency fund support every month, the EMI structure needs to change, not the emergency fund

The emergency fund only works if it is left alone until a genuine emergency arrives. An emergency fund that is regularly raided for non-emergencies is not an emergency fund — it is a slowly depleting savings account.

Build it before you invest aggressively

The right order of financial operations:

  1. Get term insurance if you have dependents
  2. Get health insurance independent of employer cover
  3. Build a 3-month emergency fund
  4. Start equity SIPs (even small)
  5. Build the emergency fund to 6 months while SIPs continue
  6. Step up SIPs with every income increase thereafter

Steps 1–3 do not need to be fully complete before starting step 4. A ₹2,000 SIP started while building the emergency fund is better than waiting until the emergency fund is "complete". But term insurance and at least a minimal health cover should be in place before significant investment begins.

Why this is the most skipped part of personal finance

Insurance premiums feel like money paid for nothing — you get no statement showing your money compounding, no NAV to check, no sense of progress. If nothing goes wrong, the premium feels wasted. This is exactly backwards. The premium buys the certainty that if something does go wrong, the financial damage is contained. That certainty has enormous value — it just does not appear in any portfolio tracker.

Emergency funds feel passive and boring compared to an equity portfolio. A liquid fund earning 6.5% does not have the same appeal as a mid-cap fund that might return 20%. The emergency fund's purpose is not to maximise return. Its purpose is to be there unconditionally. A mid-cap fund that is down 30% when you need it urgently is not an emergency fund, regardless of its long-term return.

The cost of not having these in place

Consider what happens without each of them. Without term insurance: your family loses the income that funds their life, the home loan, the school fees, the parents' support — all at once, without any preparation. Without health insurance: a serious illness depletes savings and investments built over a decade, in months. Without an emergency fund: a job loss forces you to sell equity in a correction, locking in losses and starting over at zero. Each of these scenarios is common. None of them is unusual or unlikely. The protection cost is small. The cost of not having it is potentially total.

None of these appear in your portfolio tracker. None of them generate returns you can plot on a chart. All three are prerequisites to long-term investing working as intended — because a single event without them can undo everything the portfolio was built to achieve.
