Loss Aversion: Why a 20% Loss Hurts More Than a 20% Gain Feels Good

The pain of losing money is psychologically about twice as powerful as the pleasure of gaining the same amount. This asymmetry is not a character flaw. It is how human brains are wired. Understanding it is the first step to not letting it destroy your returns.

The research behind loss aversion

Behavioural economists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky documented that losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel pleasurable. A ₹1 lakh loss causes approximately twice the psychological pain as a ₹1 lakh gain causes pleasure.

This was not designed to hurt investors. It evolved as a survival mechanism — avoiding losses was more important than finding gains when the stakes were physical survival. In the context of long-term investing, however, this hardwired response consistently produces the wrong decisions.

How loss aversion shows up in investing

1

Exiting at the bottom

A portfolio falls 25%. The pain is intense. Every day of further holding feels like more loss. The decision to exit feels like stopping the bleeding. In reality, the exit locks in the loss and removes participation from the recovery.

2

Waiting too long to re-enter

After exiting during a fall, the investor waits for clarity before re-entering. Clarity arrives when prices have already recovered significantly. They re-enter at prices higher than where they exited, having experienced the full loss and none of the recovery.

3

Holding losing funds too long

Loss aversion also works in reverse: investors hold underperforming funds longer than they should because selling realises the loss and makes it feel real. An unrealised loss still feels like a temporary situation that might reverse.

4

Reducing SIPs when markets fall

The same pain response that drives exit decisions also drives SIP pauses. Continuing to invest when the portfolio is down feels like adding to a loss. In reality, those are the months when each SIP buys the most units at the cheapest price.

The actual cost in return terms

Loss aversion is not just a feeling. It has a measurable impact on returns. Research consistently shows that the average investor earns 3 to 5 percentage points less per year than the funds they invest in, almost entirely because of behaviour driven by loss aversion during market corrections.

On a ₹50 lakh portfolio over 20 years, a 4% annual behaviour gap compounds to the difference between ₹2.2 crore and ₹3.7 crore at 12% and 16% respectively. The gap is not caused by fund selection. It is caused by what the investor does when the portfolio falls.

The structural solution

The antidote to loss aversion is not willpower. Telling yourself to feel less pain when the portfolio falls does not work. The solution is structural: remove the conditions that trigger the pain response.

  • Check less frequently: daily monitoring maximises exposure to short-term losses. Quarterly monitoring dramatically reduces the emotional input.
  • Use goal context: when the portfolio falls, the question is not "how much have I lost?" It is "is my retirement goal still on track?" For a goal 18 years away, the answer is almost always yes — and that answer reduces the pain stimulus.
  • Automate SIPs: if the SIP runs automatically, the decision to continue does not have to be made in the moment of pain. Automation removes the choice.
  • Pre-commit to staying in: write down your investment plan and the conditions under which you will change it (hint: market falls are not on the list). Reviewing what you wrote during a correction provides an anchor against emotional decisions.
