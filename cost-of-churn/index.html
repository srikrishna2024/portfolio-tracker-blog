The True Cost of Portfolio Churn

Switching funds feels like active management. The data shows it is usually the opposite of that.

What churn actually looks like

Portfolio churn is not just selling everything and starting over. It shows up in subtler patterns:

  • Switching from a fund that underperformed last year to one that outperformed last year
  • Adding a new "promising" fund every time a category does well
  • Stopping SIPs during corrections and restarting when markets recover
  • Switching to direct plans after years of regular plans, triggering unnecessary redemptions
  • Consolidating from 12 funds to 5 funds, then expanding back to 9 funds two years later

Each of these feels like a rational, considered decision at the time. The cost only becomes visible in aggregate.

The three costs of switching

💸

Exit loads and taxes

Most equity funds charge 1% exit load if redeemed within one year. STCG tax of 20% applies on gains from units held less than 12 months. LTCG tax of 12.5% applies above ₹1.25 lakhs for longer-held units. A switch that feels like moving from a 14% fund to a 16% fund may net you less after these costs, especially in the first few years.

📉

Buying high, selling low

The timing of most fund switches is driven by recent performance. Investors sell funds that have underperformed and buy funds that have recently done well. Data consistently shows that last year's outperformer tends to mean-revert. You sell at a relative low and buy at a relative high, even though both are described in terms of their past returns.

🧮

Compounding interrupted

The deepest cost of churn is not visible in any transaction. It is the compounding that did not happen because money sat in a liquid fund or savings account during the transition. A three-month gap in a 20-year investment horizon costs more than it appears. Compounding is not linear. The later years do the heavy lifting, and every interruption shortens the effective compounding period.

How the behavioural gap actually forms

DALBAR, a US financial research firm, has tracked investor returns vs fund returns for decades. The finding is consistent: the average investor earns significantly less than the funds they invest in, because they buy and sell at the wrong times.

Indian investors face the same pattern. A fund may deliver 15% CAGR over a decade. An investor who entered and exited that fund twice in response to performance cycles may have earned 9%. The fund did its job. The investor got in the way of it.

This is not a criticism of investors. It is a structural problem. When you have no framework for evaluating whether a fund is doing its job, performance charts are the only signal available. And recent performance is the worst signal you can use.

What a better signal looks like

Instead of "has this fund returned more or less than another fund recently?", better questions are:

  • Is this fund consistently tracking its benchmark with low tracking error (K4)?
  • Given this fund's historical worst-case returns, is my goal still safely achievable (K8)?
  • Has this fund's behaviour changed since a new manager took over?
  • Is this fund actually running a different strategy than its category label suggests?

These questions take more than 30 seconds to answer. That is intentional. Switching should require effort. The best fund decisions are ones you are still confident about a year later, not ones driven by a quarter of underperformance.

The case for a boring portfolio

Three to five funds, consistent SIPs, annual review. That is the portfolio that outperforms most actively managed approaches over a decade, not because the fund selection is better, but because the investor behaviour is better.
