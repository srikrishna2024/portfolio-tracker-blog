The most common piece of advice given to equity investors during a market crash is: "Keep your SIP going. Rupee cost averaging will take care of it."

This advice is correct in the early years of accumulation. It becomes dangerously misleading in the years before a goal.

Understanding why — and precisely when the advice stops applying — is one of the most important things a goal-based investor can internalize.

---

## What Equity Dampening Actually Is

When markets fall, a continuing SIP buys more units at a lower NAV. This lowers your average cost per unit across all the investments you have made. When markets eventually recover, you recover faster and with more units than you would have if you had stopped investing or invested only at the peak.

This is the equity dampening effect — the way regular investing through a crash *dampens* the impact of the crash on your long-term returns.

The effect is real, measurable, and powerful. In the early years of accumulation it is one of the most valuable things working in your favour. The problem is that it depends entirely on one condition:

**Your monthly SIP must be large relative to your accumulated corpus.**

When this condition holds, the dampening effect is strong. When it breaks down — as it inevitably does as the corpus grows — the protection disappears.

---

## The Arithmetic of Why It Breaks Down

Let us work through the numbers precisely.

**Year 2 of accumulation**

Suppose you have been investing ₹20,000 per month for 2 years. At a 10% annual return, your corpus is approximately ₹5.3L.

A 40% market crash reduces your corpus to approximately ₹3.2L — a loss of ₹2.1L.

Your monthly SIP is ₹20,000. As a percentage of the damage: 20,000 / 2,10,000 = roughly 10% per month.

Within 10 months of buying at depressed NAV, your SIP contributions have already replaced the principal loss in rupee terms — before the market has even recovered. By the time the market returns to its previous level, you hold significantly more units than you would have if the crash had never happened. Your SIP did enormous work.

**Year 10 of accumulation**

Now suppose you have been investing ₹20,000 per month for 10 years. At a 10% annual return, your corpus is approximately ₹41L.

The same 40% market crash reduces your corpus to approximately ₹24.6L — a loss of ₹16.4L.

Your monthly SIP is still ₹20,000. As a percentage of the damage: 20,000 / 16,40,000 = roughly 1.2% per month.

You would need 82 months — nearly 7 years — of SIP contributions at the depressed NAV just to replace the principal loss in rupee terms. And this assumes no further market movement, which is not realistic.

The SIP is no longer doing meaningful work against the damage. It is a rounding error compared to the corpus loss.

---

## The Crossover Point

The crossover happens when your accumulated corpus grows large enough that the monthly SIP becomes insignificant relative to the potential drawdown.

There is no single universal crossover date because it depends on:
- The size of the monthly SIP
- The growth rate of the corpus
- The severity of the crash being modelled

But for a typical Indian retail investor — monthly SIP of ₹10,000–₹25,000, equity return of 10–12% — the crossover typically occurs somewhere between **years 8 and 12** of continuous investing.

You can calculate your own crossover. The question to ask is:

> If my corpus dropped 40% today, how many months of SIP contributions would it take to replace the rupee loss in absolute terms?

When the answer is more than 24 months (2 years), the SIP dampening effect has largely evaporated. You are past the crossover.

---

## Why This Matters for Goal-Based Investing

The equity dampening effect is one of the three reasons why high-volatility equity is appropriate in the early years of accumulation.

The other two reasons are time for recovery and the long-term average return advantage. Together, these three forces make equity appropriate when the goal is far away.

As you approach the goal, all three forces weaken:

1. **Time for recovery shrinks** — covered in the [recovery-time framework](/portfolio-tracker-blog/blog/2026/06/fund-category-timeline-matching/)
2. **Return advantage narrows** — over shorter windows, the range of equity return outcomes widens and the probability of underperforming debt increases
3. **SIP dampening diminishes** — the focus of this post

The important practical point is that the SIP dampening effect does not just weaken gradually. It crosses a threshold beyond which it is essentially gone. After the crossover, when someone says "keep your SIP going through the crash, it will average down," they are giving advice that is numerically correct but practically irrelevant.

The corpus is not going to be saved by rupee cost averaging at this point. It needs *protection*, not averaging.

---

## The Corpus-Dominated Phase

After the crossover, you have entered what can be called the corpus-dominated phase — where the existing accumulated wealth, not the new monthly contributions, determines your outcome at the goal date.

In this phase, the questions that matter change:

| Before crossover | After crossover |
|---|---|
| "Can I keep investing through crashes?" | "Is my corpus protected from crashes?" |
| "How much am I buying at low NAV?" | "How much is my corpus exposed to drawdown?" |
| "What is my long-term average return?" | "What is my corpus worth on the goal date?" |

The advice appropriate for each phase is different. "Keep your SIP going through crashes" is excellent advice for the before-crossover phase. It is insufficient — and potentially dangerous — advice for the after-crossover phase.

---

## The Practical Implication: Two Portfolios, Not One

One way to think about this is that a long-horizon goal-based investor is effectively running two portfolios at different points in time — they just do not recognise it.

**Portfolio Phase 1 (early accumulation, before crossover):**
High equity is appropriate. SIP averaging is a genuine protection mechanism. Crashes are beneficial in the long run. The focus is on maximising long-term return while maintaining SIP discipline.

**Portfolio Phase 2 (late accumulation, after crossover):**
The focus shifts from return maximisation to corpus protection. Equity is still present but in lower and declining amounts. The primary tool for managing risk is not averaging — it is asset allocation, specifically reducing the amount of corpus exposed to equity volatility.

The mistake most investors make is running Portfolio Phase 2 with Portfolio Phase 1 logic. They keep the same equity allocation they had when the SIP dampening was working, into a phase where the corpus is large, the goal is close, and the SIP can no longer protect them.

---

## A Common Mistake: "I Have Been Investing for 12 Years, Equity Has Always Worked Out"

This is the survivorship bias of personal experience meeting the crossover problem.

An investor who has held equity for 12 years has experienced the *early accumulation phase* — the phase where SIP averaging, long recovery windows, and the equity return premium all work together. Their experience of equity during this phase has been broadly positive, crashes and all.

But their next phase — the 5–10 years before major goals — is categorically different. The same equity allocation that served them well in years 1–12 is now exposed to risks that the SIP cannot cushion.

The evidence from their own history does not apply to the phase they are entering. The phase they are entering has different risk characteristics because *the size of their corpus has changed everything.*

---

## What to Do Instead

The equity dampening effect diminishing does not mean stopping SIPs. It means shifting what the SIP is doing.

In the early phase, the SIP is doing two things: building the corpus and dampening equity risk. Both are valuable.

In the later phase, the SIP should increasingly flow into debt, not equity. The corpus protection comes not from averaging down in equity but from having a larger proportion of the corpus in assets that do not drawdown 40% in a crash.

Practically:
- Continue SIPs throughout the investment journey
- As the goal approaches 10 years, begin redirecting new SIP contributions toward debt — not because equity is bad, but because the corpus protection need has replaced the equity averaging benefit
- The *equity* SIP can be maintained or reduced; the key is that the *total savings rate* continues and the marginal rupee goes toward the protection-phase asset class

This is the two-dial framework applied to monthly contributions: just as the equity *percentage* should decline as goals approach, the equity *SIP amount* should also gradually reduce as a proportion of total monthly savings.

## How to Know If You Have Crossed Over — The One Annual Check

You do not need to calculate the crossover precisely. You need a simple signal you can check once a year.

**The crossover has happened when your corpus falling 1% hurts you more in rupees than your entire month's SIP helps you.**

### The One Ratio to Track

**Ratio = Monthly SIP ÷ Total Corpus**

Calculate this once a year. It takes two minutes.

| Ratio | Zone | What It Means |
|---|---|---|
| Above 2% | SIP dominant | Averaging effect is strong. Aggressive equity is fine. |
| 1%–2% | Transition | SIP weakening but still meaningful. Begin glide path thinking. |
| Below 1% | Corpus dominant | Crossover confirmed. Portfolio structure must manage risk now. |
| Below 0.5% | Well past crossover | Glide path execution is urgent, not optional. |

### The Crossover Number in Plain English

The crossover happens when the ratio hits 1% — which means corpus = 100 times your monthly SIP.

- Monthly SIP ₹20,000 → crossover at ₹20 lakh corpus
- Monthly SIP ₹30,000 → crossover at ₹30 lakh corpus
- Monthly SIP ₹50,000 → crossover at ₹50 lakh corpus

Write your crossover number down today. Check your corpus against it once a year.

### Two Real Examples

**Ravi — Year 3**
Monthly SIP: ₹20,000 · Corpus: ₹10 lakh · Ratio: **2.0%**

A 1% fall in corpus = ₹1,000. His SIP is 20x that. SIP is enormously powerful. Well pre-crossover. Hold aggressive equity confidently.

**Suresh — Year 12**
Monthly SIP: ₹20,000 · Corpus: ₹50 lakh · Ratio: **0.4%**

A 1% fall in corpus = ₹5,000. His SIP covers only 4 months of a single 1% move — and markets can fall 1% in a day. Well past crossover. Portfolio structure must do the heavy lifting now.

### The Three Zones (For ₹20,000 SIP)

| Zone | Corpus Range | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| SIP dominant | Below ₹10 lakh (below 50x SIP) | Hold aggressive equity. SIP is your shock absorber. |
| Transition | ₹10–30 lakh (50x–150x SIP) | Begin glide path. Redirect new flows toward debt. No new mid/small cap. |
| Corpus dominant | Above ₹30 lakh (above 150x SIP) | Execute glide path actively. Large cap only if equity held at all. |

### The Annual Check — Two Steps

On a fixed date every year (your investment anniversary, April 1st — whatever works):

1. Note your total equity corpus value
2. Divide your monthly SIP by that number

Read the result against the table above. That is the entire check.

### One Important Caveat

The crossover ratio can temporarily improve during a crash — a corpus that was ₹50 lakh at Zone 3 becomes ₹30 lakh after a 40% crash, pushing the ratio back toward Zone 2. Do not use this as a reason to hold or add aggressive equity. The ratio improved only because your corpus shrank, which is not a good thing. During a crash, the glide path schedule overrides the ratio.

>

>
> **Below it — SIP is my shock absorber. Aggressive equity is fine.**
>

## The Honest Summary

The SIP averaging benefit — rupee cost averaging, equity dampening — is one of the most powerful forces in personal finance. It genuinely protects early-stage investors through market crashes and is a strong argument for staying invested.

But it has a lifespan. It works when the monthly contribution is large relative to the corpus. It stops working when the corpus becomes large relative to the monthly contribution.

For most Indian retail investors, this crossover happens sometime in the 8–12 year range. After this point, corpus protection — not SIP averaging — is the primary risk management tool.

**"Keep your SIP going through crashes" is correct advice for years 1–8 and potentially harmful advice for years 12–15 before a goal.** The same words mean different things depending on where you are in the accumulation cycle.

Knowing which phase you are in is not optional for anyone serious about goal-based investing.
