Active Funds, Passive Funds, and Peace of Mind

The active vs passive debate is usually framed as a question of returns. It is more usefully framed as a question of what you can sleep with for 20 years.

What each side actually promises

An index fund makes one promise: to deliver the benchmark return, minus a small expense ratio. No more, no less. When the Nifty 50 returns 12% in a year, you get roughly 12% minus 0.1 to 0.2%. When it falls 25%, you fall 25%. The outcome is completely predictable given the market outcome.

An active fund makes a different promise: to deliver a return better than the benchmark through skill in stock selection and timing. This promise has a cost — typically 1 to 2% per year in expense ratio — and a variable outcome. In a good cycle, an active fund can return 18% when the benchmark returned 12%. In a bad cycle, it can return 8% when the benchmark returned 12%. Over 20 years, the compounded cost of underperformance years can be significant.

Neither promise is dishonest. They are just different products with different risk profiles, different costs, and different emotional experiences for the investor holding them.

The case for passive

The strongest argument for index funds is not that active managers are incompetent. It is that the average active manager, after fees, underperforms the index over long periods. This is arithmetic, not opinion: the average active fund return must equal the average index return before fees, because active funds collectively hold the market. After fees, they must collectively underperform.

Some active funds beat the index consistently. They are genuinely rare, and identifying them in advance — rather than buying last year's winner — is the real challenge. For most investors who cannot spend the time to evaluate manager skill, rolling consistency, and AUM trends, a low-cost index fund removes the selection problem entirely.

Index funds also have a significant behavioural advantage. When an active fund underperforms its benchmark for two years, investors face pressure to switch. The underperformance feels like evidence of a broken strategy. With an index fund, two years of underperformance relative to… the index is impossible. The fund is the index. There is nothing to question, nothing to switch from, nothing to second-guess. This removes a significant source of self-inflicted performance drag.

The case for active

The Indian equity market has characteristics that make the active vs passive question less settled than in the US. Mid-cap and small-cap markets in India are less efficiently priced than large-caps. Information about smaller companies is less widely distributed, giving skilled analysts a genuine edge that disappears in large-cap markets where thousands of analysts cover the same stocks.

For large-cap exposure, the evidence for active management in India has weakened significantly as markets have become more efficient and as index funds have become available with very low expense ratios. For mid-cap and small-cap exposure, the evidence is more mixed — there are managers with genuine long-term track records of delivering alpha in these segments.

The Factor Analysis page in Personal Portfolio Tracker measures this directly for each fund you hold: R² shows how closely a fund tracks its benchmark, and tracking error shows how much it deviates. A large-cap active fund with R² of 0.97 is functionally an expensive index fund. A mid-cap active fund with lower R² and a genuine 10-year track record of beating its benchmark may be worth the higher expense ratio.

How to decide for your own portfolio

1

Check what you actually own

Many investors holding "active" large-cap funds are holding closet index funds — high R², high expense ratio, mediocre long-term alpha. The Factor Analysis page shows this clearly. If your active fund has an R² above 0.95 against its benchmark, you are paying active fees for index-like returns. Switching to an index fund in that category would reduce cost without meaningfully changing your exposure.

2

Measure consistency, not last year's return

For active funds you decide to keep, the Fund Performance page shows rolling return consistency across multiple periods. A fund that beats its benchmark median in 7 out of 10 rolling 3-year windows is a different proposition than one that had one exceptional year three years ago. Consistency over a full market cycle is the only evidence that matters.

3

Match the strategy to the category

A simple structure that many investors find effective: passive (index fund) for large-cap exposure where market efficiency is high, active for mid-cap where genuine alpha opportunities exist. This is not a universal rule — it is a starting framework. The Correlation page shows whether the active and passive funds you hold are actually adding diversification or duplicating exposure.

4

Know what you can hold through a crash

An active mid-cap fund that has returned 18% over 5 years can fall 40% in a crash. An index fund does the same. The question is not which one falls less — it is which one you will actually hold through the fall without selling. A fund you understand and believe in is one you are more likely to keep investing in when markets are down. That decision — to keep the SIP running through the fall — is worth more than the active vs passive choice.

The peace of mind calculation

The best portfolio is the one that produces the best outcome for your goals over 20 years. That is partly a question of fund selection. It is mostly a question of investor behaviour — specifically, whether you kept investing through corrections, did not switch funds based on short-term performance, and let compounding do its work.

Index funds tend to win the behaviour test because there is nothing to second-guess. Active funds can win the returns test if you choose well and hold through the full cycle. Both can fail if you react to short-term noise and switch repeatedly.

The Behaviour Analytics page in Personal Portfolio Tracker computes your actual behaviour gap — the difference between your portfolio's return and what you would have earned if you had simply stayed invested. For most investors, this gap is larger than the active vs passive alpha difference. Fix the behaviour gap first. Then optimise the fund selection.
