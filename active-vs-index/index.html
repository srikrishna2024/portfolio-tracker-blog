Active Funds vs Index Funds: Do You Get What You Pay For?

Active funds charge more. The question is whether they deliver enough extra return to justify the cost. The answer depends on which fund, which category, and which time period. You can now measure it yourself.

What you actually pay for active management

A typical actively managed equity fund in India charges an expense ratio of 1 to 1.5% per year on the direct plan. A comparable index fund charges 0.1 to 0.2%. The difference, roughly 1 to 1.3% per year, is what you pay for a fund manager to research, select, and time stocks differently from the index.

That 1% difference sounds small. It is not.

How 1% annual fee compounds over 20 years:

Suppose you invest ₹50 lakhs today and it grows at 12% CAGR in an index fund. After 20 years: approximately ₹4.8 crore.

The same investment in an active fund growing at 12% CAGR but with 1% higher fees effectively grows at 11%. After 20 years: approximately ₹4.0 crore.

The difference: roughly ₹80 lakhs. Not because the market performed differently, but entirely because of fees.

For the active fund to justify its fee, it must not just match the index after fees. It must beat it by enough to also compensate for this compounding cost. Every year. Across market cycles.

This is the hurdle. The question is how often active funds clear it, and whether yours does.

What the data in your own database shows

You do not need to rely on external research reports. Personal Portfolio Tracker downloads NAV history for every mutual fund from AMFI, going back 10 to 15 years for established funds. That data is sitting in your local database right now. You can run the Consistency Screener on any category and see the distribution yourself.

What you will typically find when you run it:

  • In most categories, the distribution of 3Y consistency scores spreads from around 30% to 90%. The median fund sits close to 50%, barely better than a coin flip.
  • The funds at the top of the consistency ranking tend to stay near the top across different rolling periods. Persistence of skill is real but rare.
  • Many funds with high 1-year returns have moderate or low consistency scores. The headline return came from a concentrated period, not sustained outperformance.
  • Downside capture varies more than most investors expect. Within the same category, one fund might fall only 80% as much as the benchmark on bad days while another falls 115% as much. Both charge similar fees.

The scatter plot in the Consistency Screener makes this visible immediately. Run it for your category and look at where most dots cluster. If the majority of dots sit below 65% on the X-axis, the category is difficult to beat consistently, and an index fund becomes the rational default. If a cluster of funds sits above 70% consistency with downside capture below 95%, the category rewards skilled active management.

This is your data, computed from AMFI's public records. No third-party reports, no survivorship bias from fund houses cherry-picking their best periods. You see every fund that currently exists in the category, ranked on the same objective criteria.

The consistency problem

The most misleading thing about active fund performance is that it is evaluated on a single number: last year's return, or 3-year CAGR. That number tells you almost nothing useful.

A fund that returned 18% last year might have done so because its concentrated bet on one sector paid off. The same bet that worked in year one may reverse in year two. The fund's high return was not a demonstration of sustainable skill. It was a demonstration of a particular risk being rewarded.

The right question is not "what did this fund return?" It is "how consistently did this fund beat its benchmark across many different market conditions?" A fund that beat the benchmark in 75% of all 3-year rolling windows starting from different dates is demonstrating something more durable than a fund that had one extraordinary 3-year period.

The two tests that matter

If you are going to pay active fund fees, two things must be true simultaneously:

1

Consistent outperformance

The fund should beat its benchmark in a large majority of rolling return windows, not just in one favourable period. If a fund has a 3-year consistency score of 65% or higher over a 10-year history, it has demonstrated genuine ability to add value across different market conditions, not just lucky timing.

A consistency score below 50% means the fund beats the benchmark less than half the time. You would do as well or better with an index fund and save the fees.

2

Downside protection

An active fund that beats the benchmark on good days but falls more on bad days is not adding value. It is just taking more risk. A fund with a downside capture ratio above 100% means it falls more than the index when markets drop. You are paying active fees to take more risk, which is the opposite of what active management should do.

A fund with downside capture below 90%, meaning it falls only 90% as much as the benchmark on bad days, is genuinely protecting your capital. Over a long horizon, this loss mitigation compounds into significantly better outcomes even if the upside capture is modest.

A fund that scores well on both, consistent outperformance across rolling windows and lower downside capture, is earning its fee. A fund that scores well on only one is marginal. A fund that scores poorly on both is a strong candidate for replacement with an index fund.

Which categories favour active management in India

The case for active management varies by category:

Large Cap: Hardest category to beat consistently

Large-cap stocks are extensively covered. Run the Consistency Screener on the Large Cap category with a 5Y rolling period. You will see that the median consistency score tends to cluster around 50 to 60%, and funds with genuine 70%+ consistency and downside protection below 95% are rare. Where they exist, they are worth keeping. Where they do not, a NIFTY 50 or NIFTY 100 index fund at 0.1 to 0.2% expense ratio is the rational default.

Mid Cap: More opportunity for skilled active managers

Less covered, more volatile, more opportunity for stock-picking edge. Run the screener here and you will typically find a wider spread: some funds with strong consistency scores and good downside protection, many with inconsistent records. The ones that pass both tests are worth the fee. The rest are candidates for a mid-cap index fund.

Flexi Cap: Depends entirely on how the manager uses flexibility

The theoretical advantage is active allocation across market caps. Whether a specific fund uses this or just holds a closet large-cap portfolio is what the Factor Analysis tool reveals (R² close to 1 against a large-cap index = closet indexer). Use both tools together: Factor Analysis to check if the fund is genuinely active, Consistency Screener to check if that activity is adding value.

Small Cap: Is the extra risk worth it?

Small-cap funds offer the theoretical highest opportunity for active outperformance. But the drawdowns are severe, recovery times are long, and the volatility extracts a significant psychological toll. A 50% fall in a small-cap fund is not unusual in a bad year. Most investors significantly underestimate how that feels when it happens.

More importantly: your financial goals do not require small-cap returns. A well-constructed portfolio of large-cap and mid-cap funds, with consistent SIPs and a long horizon, will meet most retirement and goal targets. Small cap adds complexity, concentration risk, and stress without adding a proportionate improvement to outcomes for the average investor.

If you do hold small-cap funds, run the Consistency Screener with special attention to downside capture. A small-cap fund with downside capture above 110% is amplifying the index's already-severe volatility. That is not an investment. It is speculation with active management fees on top.

How to use the Consistency Screener to decide

Personal Portfolio Tracker's Consistency Screener gives you the data to answer this question for your specific funds in your specific categories. Here is how to use it:

  1. Go to Fund Analysis → Consistency Screener
  2. Select the category your fund belongs to (e.g. Flexi Cap Fund)
  3. Set the rolling period to 3Y or 5Y
  4. Click Analyse and wait for the results
  5. Find your fund in the scatter plot (it appears as a ⭐ star)
  6. Check: is it in the top-right quadrant (above 65% consistency and below 100% downside capture)?
  7. Look at the rolling returns chart. Does your fund's line consistently sit above the benchmark dashed line, or does it only beat the benchmark in specific periods?

If your fund is top-right with a rolling returns chart that genuinely tracks above the benchmark through multiple market cycles, it is earning its fee. If it is bottom-left, or if the rolling chart shows it only outperformed in one particular bull or bear market, it is a candidate for replacement with an index fund in that category.

A practical approach to using these findings

The goal is not to pick a side, all active or all index, but to make the decision consciously for each holding, based on evidence rather than habit or distributor recommendation.

1

Run the Consistency Screener on every category you hold

Select the category, set rolling period to 3Y or 5Y, and click Analyse. Look at where your fund sits in the scatter plot. If your fund is in the top-right quadrant (high consistency + low downside capture) among all peers, it is earning its fee. If it is bottom-left, it is not.

2

Look at the rolling returns chart

Does your fund's line consistently sit above the benchmark dashed line across multiple market cycles? Or did it only outperform during one particular bull market or a specific sector rotation? Episodic outperformance does not justify an ongoing annual fee.

3

Review once a year, not more

Do this every April as part of the annual review. A fund that fails the screener in one year gets a grace period. A fund that fails for two consecutive annual reviews is a candidate for replacement with a low-cost index fund in the same category. One bad year is noise. Two consecutive years is a pattern.

4

Keep the portfolio simple

The number of funds you hold does not determine your returns. Consistency does. Three funds that pass both tests beat ten funds of mixed quality. When in doubt, reduce to fewer, better-tested holdings rather than adding more.
