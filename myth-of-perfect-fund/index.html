The Myth of the Perfect Fund

Every year there is a top-performing fund. Every year investors chase it. The math of what happens next is consistent and rarely discussed.

The ranking trap

Mutual fund performance tables are one of the most misleading tools in personal finance. They rank funds by recent return, which is exactly the metric with the least predictive value for future returns.

The reason is mean reversion. A fund that ranks first in its category over 1 year has typically taken above-average concentration risk, had an above-average run in a specific sector, or benefited from a style cycle. These conditions rarely persist. The fund that ranked first this year has a statistically higher-than-average chance of ranking in the bottom half next year.

SEBI data on Indian mutual funds consistently shows that fewer than 30% of funds in the top quartile in any given year remain in the top quartile the following year. Chasing last year's top performer is, on average, a losing strategy.

What "outperformance" actually requires

For an actively managed equity fund to consistently outperform its benchmark after fees, the fund manager needs to:

  • Select stocks that outperform the index on average across hundreds of decisions
  • Do this consistently over multiple market cycles, not just in one favourable cycle
  • Overcome an expense ratio headwind of 1 to 2% per year just to break even with an index fund
  • Manage larger AUM as the fund gets popular, making the stock selection problem harder

Some fund managers do this. They are genuinely rare, and identifying them in advance, rather than in retrospect, is the actual challenge. Most "alpha" in fund performance is cycle alpha, not skill alpha. It disappears when the cycle turns.

The consistency test

Instead of asking "what has this fund returned recently?", the more useful question is "how consistently does this fund track its benchmark, and how often does it beat its category median?"

A fund that returns 14% in a year the benchmark returned 8% is interesting. A fund that beats its category median in 7 out of 10 rolling 3-year periods is useful.

Consistency matters because your goal is not to have the best return in a single year. Your goal is to accumulate a corpus over 15 or 20 years. For that, a fund that reliably captures benchmark returns with low tracking error may serve you better than one that oscillates between top and bottom quartile.

What a good fund actually looks like

1

Low tracking error

The fund behaves predictably relative to its benchmark. You know what you are getting. A low K4 score in Personal Portfolio Tracker means the fund is doing its job reliably.

2

Consistent category ranking

Ranks in the top half of its category across multiple time windows, not just one. The Fund Performance page in the software shows rolling return consistency across 1Y, 3Y, and 5Y windows.

3

Stable management

The track record belongs to the manager, not just the scheme name. A fund that has had three managers in five years has a track record of fund management processes, not of a specific investment approach. Manager continuity matters.

4

Reasonable AUM

Very large mid-cap and small-cap funds face structural disadvantages: they cannot take meaningful positions in smaller companies without moving the price. Outperformance at ₹5,000 crore AUM does not guarantee outperformance at ₹50,000 crore.

The boring portfolio outperforms

Three to five funds, chosen for consistency not recent glory, held through multiple market cycles with SIPs never stopped. That portfolio, on average, outperforms the portfolio of investors who continuously optimised for last year's winners.

Not because the fund selection is better. Because the investor behaviour is better. The investors who hold boring consistent funds tend not to churn them, because there is nothing exciting enough to trigger a switch.

The best thing a fund can do for your retirement is be boring and reliable for 20 years.
