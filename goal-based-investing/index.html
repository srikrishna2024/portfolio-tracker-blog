Goal-Based Investing: A Practical Primer

Most investors think about their portfolio as one pool of money. That single change in thinking is why goal-based investing produces better outcomes.

The problem with treating everything as one portfolio

Imagine you have ₹40 lakhs invested across five mutual funds. You check your portfolio app. It says your XIRR is 13.4%. The question is: is that good or bad?

You cannot answer that question without knowing what the money is for.

If ₹20 lakhs of it is earmarked for your daughter's college in 4 years, 13.4% XIRR may be completely insufficient. If ₹15 lakhs is for retirement in 22 years, 13.4% may be more than enough. The same number tells you opposite things depending on the goal.

Treating everything as one portfolio creates a specific kind of blindness: you can be doing well on average while a critical goal is silently falling behind.

What goal-based investing actually means

Goal-based investing means assigning every rupee you invest to a specific purpose with a specific deadline and a specific target amount. It has three practical effects:

1

You know whether you are on track

Instead of asking "is my portfolio doing well?", you ask "is this goal on track?". The answer is concrete: yes, or short by ₹X, close the gap with ₹Y per month more.

2

Each goal gets the right risk level

A goal 20 years away can take more equity risk than a goal 3 years away. When goals are separated, each gets an allocation appropriate to its timeline, not a one-size-fits-all portfolio allocation.

3

Market falls feel different

When the market drops 30%, a goal-based investor asks: "is my retirement goal still on track given this fall?". Often the answer is yes, because the goal is 18 years away. The panic that drives bad decisions comes from not knowing the answer to that question.

How to set realistic targets

The most common mistake in goal setting is underestimating future costs due to inflation. A goal that costs ₹50 lakhs today will cost more in 15 years. Here is a rough guide:

Goal typeKey variableCommon mistake
Retirement corpus Monthly expenses at retirement, inflated forward Using today's expenses without inflation. A ₹80,000/month lifestyle today needs ₹2+ lakhs/month in 20 years at 5% inflation.
Child's education Current cost of target college, education inflation 8 to 10% Using today's fee structure. Engineering or MBA costs double roughly every 8 years.
House purchase Target property value, down payment percentage Assuming a fixed target. Real estate prices in most Indian cities have risen faster than general inflation.
Emergency fund 6 to 12 months of monthly expenses Setting it once and forgetting to update as expenses grow. Review annually.

Why mixing goals into one portfolio creates confusion

When all your money is in one portfolio, rebalancing becomes emotionally difficult. If you need to reduce equity exposure for a short-term goal approaching, you have to sell from the same pool that is also funding your 20-year retirement. Every decision feels like it affects everything.

Separate goal tracking does not mean separate bank accounts or separate demat accounts. It means knowing, within your unified portfolio, which funds are doing which job. A single mid-cap fund can serve retirement. A debt fund can serve the house down payment in 3 years. The mapping is in the software, not in separate physical accounts.

Once that mapping exists, every question becomes answerable: Should I step up my SIP? By how much, for which goal? Should I rebalance? Which goal is out of alignment? Should I worry about this correction? Which goal's timeline is affected?

The sequence of returns risk nobody talks about

One of the most underappreciated risks in investing is sequence of returns risk: the order in which returns arrive matters enormously for goals with fixed deadlines.

Consider two investors who both earn 12% CAGR over 10 years but in different sequences. Investor A gets strong early returns and weak late returns. Investor B gets weak early returns and strong late returns. If both are accumulating, they end up at the same place. But if Investor A is approaching retirement and hits bad returns in the last 3 years while withdrawing, the outcome is permanently worse.

Goal-based investing addresses this by shifting allocation towards safer assets as each goal approaches. When your retirement is 20 years away, equity volatility is your friend. When it is 3 years away, it is your enemy. The glide path built into Personal Portfolio Tracker tracks this automatically for each goal, not for your portfolio as a whole.

Starting with just one goal

You do not need to have every goal perfectly defined before you start. Start with the one that matters most or the one you are most uncertain about. Often that is retirement, because the corpus required is large and the SIP required is surprisingly high when you calculate it properly.

Once you see the retirement numbers clearly, the discipline to stay invested through volatility tends to follow naturally. You are no longer watching a number go up and down. You are watching a goal either stay on track or fall behind, and the required action is obvious.
