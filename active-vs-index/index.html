Active Funds vs Index Funds: Do You Get What You Pay For?

Active funds charge more. The question is whether they deliver enough extra return to justify the cost. The answer depends on which fund, which category, and which time period — and you can now measure it yourself.

What you actually pay for active management

A typical actively managed equity fund in India charges an expense ratio of 1 to 1.5% per year on the direct plan. A comparable index fund charges 0.1 to 0.2%. The difference — roughly 1 to 1.3% per year — is what you pay for a fund manager to research, select, and time stocks differently from the index.

On a ₹50 lakh portfolio over 20 years, 1% extra annual cost compounds to roughly ₹25 to 30 lakhs of additional fees paid. That is not a small number. The active fund needs to earn back that difference every year, plus deliver actual outperformance on top of it, just to break even with an index fund.

This is the hurdle. The question is how often active funds clear it.

  • The funds at the top of the consistency ranking tend to stay near the top across different rolling periods. Persistence of skill is real but rare.
  • Many funds with high 1-year returns have moderate or low consistency scores — the headline return came from a concentrated period, not sustained outperformance.
  • Downside capture varies more than most investors expect. Within the same category, one fund might fall only 80% as much as the benchmark on bad days while another falls 115% as much. Both charge similar fees.

The consistency problem

The most misleading thing about active fund performance is that it is evaluated on a single number — last year's return, or 3-year CAGR. That number tells you almost nothing useful.

A fund that returned 18% last year might have done so because its concentrated bet on one sector paid off. The same bet that worked in year one may reverse in year two. The fund's high return was not a demonstration of sustainable skill — it was a demonstration of a particular risk being rewarded.

The right question is not "what did this fund return?" It is "how consistently did this fund beat its benchmark across many different market conditions?" A fund that beat the benchmark in 75% of all 3-year rolling windows starting from different dates is demonstrating something more durable than a fund that had one extraordinary 3-year period.

The two tests that matter

If you are going to pay active fund fees, two things must be true simultaneously:

1

Consistent outperformance

The fund should beat its benchmark in a large majority of rolling return windows — not just in one favourable period. If a fund has a 3-year consistency score of 65% or higher over a 10-year history, it has demonstrated genuine ability to add value across different market conditions, not just lucky timing.

A consistency score below 50% means the fund beats the benchmark less than half the time — you would do as well or better with an index fund and save the fees.

2

Downside protection

An active fund that beats the benchmark on good days but falls more on bad days is not adding value — it is just taking more risk. A fund with a downside capture ratio above 100% means it falls more than the index when markets drop. You are paying active fees to take more risk, which is the opposite of what active management should do.

A fund with downside capture below 90% — meaning it falls only 90% as much as the benchmark on bad days — is genuinely protecting your capital. Over a long horizon, this loss mitigation compounds into significantly better outcomes even if the upside capture is modest.

A fund that scores well on both — consistent outperformance across rolling windows AND lower downside capture — is earning its fee. A fund that scores well on only one is marginal. A fund that scores poorly on both is a strong candidate for replacement with an index fund.

Which categories favour active management in India

The case for active management varies by category:

Large Cap: Hardest category to beat consistently

Large-cap stocks are extensively covered. Run the Consistency Screener on the Large Cap category with a 5Y rolling period — you will see that the median consistency score tends to cluster around 50 to 60%, and funds with genuine 70%+ consistency and downside protection below 95% are rare. Where they exist, they are worth keeping. Where they do not, a NIFTY 50 or NIFTY 100 index fund at 0.1 to 0.2% expense ratio is the rational default.

Mid Cap: More opportunity for skilled active managers

Less covered, more volatile, more opportunity for stock-picking edge. Run the screener here and you will typically find a wider spread — some funds with strong consistency scores and good downside protection, many with inconsistent records. The ones that pass both tests are worth the fee. The rest are candidates for a mid-cap index fund.

Flexi Cap: Depends entirely on how the manager uses flexibility

The theoretical advantage is active allocation across market caps. Whether a specific fund uses this or just holds a closet large-cap portfolio is what the Factor Analysis tool reveals (R² close to 1 against a large-cap index = closet indexer). Use both tools together: Factor Analysis to check if the fund is genuinely active, Consistency Screener to check if that activity is adding value.

Small Cap: Strongest case for active, but highest risk

Most inefficient segment, most opportunity for edge. But drawdowns are severe and recovery times long. Run the screener here with a focus on downside capture — a small-cap active fund with downside capture above 110% is taking significantly more risk than the index while charging active fees. The downside capture metric matters most in this category.

A practical portfolio approach

You do not have to choose between all active or all index. A pragmatic approach for the Indian investor:

  • Large cap allocation: use a NIFTY 50 or NIFTY 100 index fund. The evidence against consistent active outperformance here is strong.
  • Mid cap allocation: run the consistency screener annually. Keep active funds that pass both tests. Replace those that fail consistently for 2+ years with a mid-cap index fund.
  • Small cap: active management justified, but hold only 1 to 2 funds. Run the consistency check every April review. Drawdowns will be severe — the downside capture metric matters most here.
  • Debt allocation: index funds (target maturity) are almost always better than active debt funds, whose category average is dragged down by credit risk events.

The annual review is the enforcement mechanism. Markets change, fund managers change, and a fund that earned its fee for 5 years may not earn it for the next 5. The Consistency Screener makes the annual check a 10-minute exercise rather than a research project.
