Why Your Withdrawal Rate in Retirement Is Not Fixed

Most retirement planning starts with a fixed withdrawal rate. That is the wrong place to start. Your withdrawal rate is a personal number that depends on your pension, your corpus, and how they change over time.

The problem with the 4% rule

The 4% rule comes from US research in the 1990s. It says: withdraw 4% of your corpus in year one of retirement, then increase the amount by inflation each year, and your money should last 30 years. It was designed for US equity and bond markets, a 30-year retirement horizon, and a 1990s interest rate environment.

None of those conditions describe an Indian retiree in 2026. Indian inflation runs higher, Indian market behaviour is different, and most Indian retirees have some combination of EPF, NPS, PPF, and Superannuation that generates income separate from the liquid portfolio. Applying the 4% rule directly gives you a number that may be significantly wrong in either direction.

The right starting point: your initial withdrawal rate

Prof. M. Pattabhiraman (freefincal) defines the Initial Withdrawal Rate (IWR) as:

IWR = (Annual Expenses − Annual Pension)Liquid Corpus at Retirement × 100

Liquid corpus excludes any amount used to purchase the pension (annuity, NPS, etc.)

A concrete example: annual retirement expenses ₹12 lakhs, pension income (EPF, NPS annuity, etc.) ₹6 lakhs per year, liquid corpus ₹1.40 crore.

IWR = (12 − 6)140 × 100 = 4.3%

This IWR tells you how much equity risk your portfolio can afford to take in retirement. A lower IWR means you need less from the corpus each year relative to its size, so you can stay invested in equity longer. A higher IWR means you are more dependent on portfolio returns, which limits how much volatility you can absorb.

Why the rate changes through retirement

Retirement is not a single uniform phase. Expenses and needs change significantly across three broad stages:

Early retirement (60 to 70)

Active years. Travel, hobbies, helping children. Expenses often higher than working years in real terms. Equity allocation can be higher because the corpus is large and recovery time exists.

Mid retirement (70 to 80)

Slower pace. Lifestyle expenses reduce. Corpus may have grown. Effective withdrawal rate often falls in this phase even with inflation, because actual spending drops.

Late retirement (80+)

Healthcare costs rise sharply. Mobility reduces. Expenses shift from discretionary to medical. Equity allocation should be much lower. Withdrawal rate may rise again.

A static 4% withdrawal assumption treats all three phases identically. The bucket strategy — splitting the corpus into short-term safe assets and long-term equity — exists precisely to handle this variation without forcing you to sell equity at the wrong time.

How to estimate your corpus requirement

A practical quick estimate, using assumptions of 7% pre-retirement inflation and 3.33% initial withdrawal rate: multiply your current annual expenses by the factor for your years to retirement.

Years to retirementMultiply current annual expenses by
15 years83x
20 years116x
25 years163x
30 years229x

If your current annual expenses are ₹3.6 lakhs (₹30,000 per month) and retirement is 25 years away: ₹3.6L × 163 = ₹587 lakhs, or approximately ₹5.87 crore needed at retirement in future rupees.

These are approximate numbers designed to give you the right order of magnitude quickly. The actual figure depends on your real inflation experience, investment returns, and pension income. But the multiplier table answers the most important question: is my current savings trajectory anywhere close to what is needed?

For most people who run this calculation for the first time, the answer is uncomfortable. That discomfort is useful — it is the motivation to step up SIPs now rather than later.
