When to Trust Your Instinct and When to Ignore It

Market falls feel like signals to act. Most of the time they are not. The skill is learning to distinguish a genuine portfolio problem from emotional noise — and having a framework that makes the distinction clear.

The problem with investor instincts

Investor instincts are trained by the wrong feedback loop. When a market falls and you exit, the market often continues to fall for a while, which feels like validation. When a fund underperforms and you switch, the new fund often does better in the short term, which also feels like validation.

What does not show up in that feedback loop: the recovery you missed, the transaction costs you paid, and the long-term return you sacrificed. These costs arrive slowly and are easy to attribute to other causes.

The result is that bad investment behaviour feels good in the short term and harmful instincts are reinforced rather than corrected.

When your instinct is right

Not every urge to act is wrong. Some situations genuinely call for a portfolio change. The key is that these situations are about the portfolio's relationship to your goals, not about market movements.

✓

Goal timeline has changed. You planned to buy a house in 5 years. You now plan to buy in 2 years. The allocation for that goal genuinely needs to shift. This is a real change in your situation, not a market reaction.

✓

Fund has been structurally underperforming for 2+ years. Not one bad quarter. Two full annual reviews showing the fund consistently in the bottom quartile against its peers with no identifiable cyclical explanation. That is a signal worth acting on.

✓

Fund manager has changed. The track record belongs to the manager, not the scheme name. A new manager is effectively a new fund. A two-year watch period is appropriate before making a decision.

✓

Your income has increased significantly. A raise or a new income stream is an instinct to act on immediately: step up SIPs before lifestyle inflation absorbs the increment.

When your instinct is noise

✗

The market fell. A 20% market correction does not change whether your retirement goal is on track for a 20-year horizon. The fall is real but the signal to exit is not.

✗

A fund returned less than another fund this year. One year of underperformance is noise in a 15-year investment. Category cycles explain most short-term divergence between similar funds.

✗

The news is uniformly negative. Market bottoms are always accompanied by uniformly negative news. This is by definition — if the news were positive, prices would not be low. Negative news at market lows is a contrary indicator, not a sell signal.

✗

Everyone else seems to be switching to something. By the time a narrative is widespread enough to create FOMO, the trade has already moved. The crowd arriving now is the exit liquidity for those who got in early.

The decision filter

Before acting on any investment instinct, run it through this filter:

  1. Has something in my life changed (income, timeline, goals) that makes this action necessary?
  2. Has something about the fund changed structurally (manager, strategy, AUM, 2+ years of consistent underperformance) rather than cyclically?
  3. Would I make this same decision if the market were flat? If the answer is no, the market is driving the decision, not the fundamentals.

If none of the three answers is yes, the instinct is probably noise. The right action is to note it, close the portfolio tracker, and come back at the next annual review.
