The [fund category to goal timeline framework](/portfolio-tracker-blog/blog/2026/06/fund-category-timeline-matching/) published earlier gives you the decision table:

| Category | Minimum timeline needed |
|---|---|
| Large cap / Index | 6–7 years minimum |
| Flexi cap (large-biased) | 7–8 years minimum |
| Mid cap | 9–10 years minimum |
| Small cap | 11–12 years minimum |

But where do these numbers come from? Why 7 and not 5 or 9? This post shows the working.

---

## The Method: Use the Bad Case, Not the Average

Before getting to the numbers, the methodology matters.

Most financial planning uses average or median return assumptions. This is wrong for goal-based planning, because a goal date is a hard deadline. You are not investing for the average outcome across thousands of investors. You are investing for *your* specific outcome on *your* specific date.

The relevant question is not "what will this fund return on average?" It is "what happens to my corpus if the worst case occurs in the years just before my goal?"

This means using the *bad case* as the planning scenario, not the average. The bad case is a major market crash with a slow recovery — specifically, a genuine economic crisis, not a sentiment-driven correction that bounces in months.

The 2020 COVID crash recovered in 6 months. That was driven by unprecedented global liquidity. It cannot be used as a planning assumption because it was exceptional.

The 2008 global financial crisis is the right scenario. It was a genuine economic disruption — housing collapse, credit freeze, institutional failures. Recovery was slow and uneven. It represents what a real bear market looks like for planning purposes.

## The Three Facts

### Fact 1 — The 2008 Drawdown and Recovery Data

Here is what actually happened to Indian equity categories during and after the 2008 crash:

**Nifty 50 (Large Cap Proxy)**
- Peak to trough: approximately 55% decline (January 2008 to March 2009)
- Recovery to previous peak: approximately 4 years (by end of 2012)

**Nifty Midcap 150 (Mid Cap Proxy)**
- Peak to trough: approximately 65% decline
- Recovery to previous peak: approximately 5–7 years (several mid cap funds took longer individually)

**Nifty Smallcap 250 (Small Cap Proxy)**
- Peak to trough: approximately 70–75% decline
- Recovery to previous peak: approximately 7–9 years for the index; individual small cap funds varied widely, with some taking 10+ years

These are not theoretical numbers. They are what happened to investors who held these categories from 2008. An investor in a small cap fund who needed the money in 2013 — 5 years after the peak — was still underwater.

**The planning implication:** The recovery time from the worst-case scenario sets the floor for how much time you need in each category.

---

### Fact 2 — The Buffer Requirement

Knowing the recovery time is not enough. You also need a buffer.

If large cap takes 4 years to recover from a 2008-scale crash, and your goal is exactly 4 years away, you are making two sequential assumptions: (a) no crash will happen in year 1, and (b) if it does, recovery will take exactly 4 years and not 5.

Both assumptions are unreliable. Markets do not know your goal date. A crash can happen in year 1 just as easily as year 3.

A rational plan builds in a buffer of at least 2–3 years beyond the worst-case recovery time. This buffer absorbs:
- Uncertainty about *when* in the goal window a crash might occur
- The possibility that recovery takes longer than the historical average
- The psychological reality that most investors will not hold perfectly through a 50% drawdown

**Buffer requirement:** Worst-case recovery time + 2–3 years = minimum timeline for that category.

For large cap: 4 years recovery + 3 years buffer = **7 years minimum**.

For mid cap: 6 years recovery + 3 years buffer = **9 years minimum**.

For small cap: 8 years recovery + 3 years buffer = **11 years minimum**.

This is where the numbers in the framework come from.

---

### Fact 3 — The SIP Averaging Crossover

The third fact is less intuitive and more important than most investors realize.

Early in accumulation, SIP averaging is a powerful force. When a crash happens in year 2 of a 15-year investment journey, your monthly SIP buys significantly more units at depressed NAV. This lowers your average cost and accelerates recovery. The SIP is doing real work.

But this effect diminishes as the corpus grows.

Here is the arithmetic. Suppose your monthly SIP is ₹20,000 and your corpus is ₹5L (after 2 years at 10% return). A 40% crash reduces the corpus by ₹2L. Your monthly SIP is 10% of the damage — it starts making a meaningful dent immediately.

Now suppose you are at year 10 and your corpus is ₹50L. The same 40% crash reduces the corpus by ₹20L. Your monthly SIP is 1% of the damage. It will take years of SIP contributions just to offset the principal loss, let alone the lost growth.

The crossover — where the accumulated corpus begins to dominate over new monthly contributions — typically happens somewhere between years 8 and 12, depending on the SIP amount and the growth rate. At this point, the equity buffering role of the SIP has largely evaporated.

This matters for the 7-year threshold in the following way: when you are 7–10 years from goal, you are likely at or near this crossover point. The SIP can no longer compensate for a major drawdown the way it once could. The corpus needs protection that equity cannot guarantee within that window.

## Putting the Three Facts Together

| Category | Worst-case recovery | + Buffer | = Minimum timeline | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Large cap / Index | 4 years | 3 years | 7 years | SIP crossover typically occurs near this point |
| Flexi cap (large-biased) | 4–5 years | 3 years | 7–8 years | Depends on actual mid-cap tilt of the fund |
| Mid cap | 5–7 years | 3 years | 8–10 years | Recovery varied significantly across 2008 funds |
| Small cap | 7–9 years | 3 years | 10–12 years | Individual fund variation was wide; use conservative end |

The 7-year number for large cap is not a precise law. It is the practical point where the combined effect of all three facts — recovery time, buffer, and SIP crossover — converges. Below 7 years, the equation shifts against holding large cap equity in a goal-linked portfolio.

---

## Why 7 and Not Something Else

The 7-year threshold for large cap comes from the *conservative* end of reasonable assumptions.

If you assumed recovery takes 3 years (faster than 2008) and buffer only 2 years, you get 5 years. Some advisors use this.

If you assumed recovery takes 5 years (slower than 2008) and buffer 3 years, you get 8 years. Some advisors use this.

The 7-year number sits in the middle of this range, using the actual 2008 data (4 years) and a 3-year buffer. It is not derived from a formula — it is a reasonable approximation that most evidence supports.

The important point is the *reasoning*, not the precise number. If you understand that the threshold comes from recovery time plus buffer, you can adjust it based on your own circumstances:

- If your goal has flexibility (can defer 1–2 years), the threshold can be slightly lower
- If your goal is non-negotiable (child's exam year, property registration date), the threshold should be slightly higher
- If you are investing a large lump sum rather than via SIP, the SIP averaging benefit is absent from the start — the threshold should be higher

## The Category-Specific Minimums

Let me be precise about what these minimums mean in practice.

**7 years for large cap** means: if your goal is more than 7 years away, large cap equity is timeline-appropriate. If it is less than 7 years, the recovery time buffer has been consumed and the fund category is now working against you rather than for you.

It does *not* mean "sell all large cap the moment you hit 7 years." It means begin the gradual migration toward debt, reducing equity exposure as described in the glide path — from 25% at 10 years to 15% at 7 years to 10% at 5 years to zero at 3 years.

**9–10 years for mid cap** means: a mid cap fund is appropriate only when you have at least 9–10 years remaining. An investor who holds a mid cap fund in a goal that is 8 years away is carrying a category where the worst-case recovery time equals the remaining window. There is no buffer.

**11–12 years for small cap** means: small cap is only appropriate when the goal is more than 11–12 years away. This eliminates small cap from the equation for most medium-term goals and means it should be one of the first things migrated away from as a goal approaches 12 years.

## The Migration Schedule in Practice

The framework implies a specific migration schedule — not based on market conditions but on the countdown to the goal date.

**When remaining time equals the minimum timeline for your current category — begin migrating to the next lower-volatility category.**

Not because the fund is underperforming. Not because markets look expensive. Because the recovery-time buffer for that category has been exhausted.

For a 20-year goal starting today:
- Years 20–12: Small cap is within its minimum timeline — appropriate
- Year 12: Begin migrating small cap to mid cap (small cap's 11-year minimum is approaching)
- Years 12–10: Mid cap within minimum timeline — appropriate
- Year 10: Begin migrating mid cap to large cap (mid cap's 9-year minimum is approaching)
- Years 10–7: Large cap within minimum timeline — appropriate
- Year 7: Begin migrating large cap to debt (large cap's 7-year minimum is approaching)
- Years 7–5: Declining large cap equity (15% to 10%)
- Year 5: Zero equity

This schedule is mechanical, not market-driven. It works precisely because it ignores market conditions — you are not trying to time the market, you are aligning your risk exposure with your remaining time buffer.

---

## The Honest Caveat

These are planning thresholds based on historical Indian market data. They are not guarantees.

A future crash could be worse than 2008 and take longer to recover. A future crash could be milder and recover faster. The framework uses the conservative end of historical data because goal-based investing optimises for certainty, not expected return.

The numbers will also be wrong for any specific fund — an individual large cap fund might take 5 years to recover when the category average is 4, or might take 2 years. The thresholds are for category-level planning, not fund-level precision.

What the framework gives you is a rational, evidence-based starting point. Adjust it based on your specific circumstances — goal flexibility, SIP size relative to corpus, and your honest assessment of how you will behave during a 40–50% drawdown.
