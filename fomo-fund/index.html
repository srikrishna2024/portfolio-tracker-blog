The FOMO Fund Problem

Every year there is a category that dominated the rankings. Every year investors pile in. The data on what happens next is consistent and rarely discussed.

How the pattern works

A category (say, small cap) has an outstanding year. It appears at the top of every return table. Financial media covers it extensively. Investors who missed the rally feel they are falling behind. New SIPs flood in.

The problem is timing. By the time a category is visible enough to generate FOMO, it has already run. The investors who benefited were those who held through the previous flat or down period. The investors arriving now are buying at elevated valuations after the easy money has been made.

This pattern repeats with remarkable consistency across categories: small cap, mid cap, thematic, sectoral, international. Each has its FOMO moment. Each subsequently mean-reverts.

What the data shows on top-quartile persistence

Research on Indian mutual fund performance shows that fewer than 30% of funds in the top quartile in any given year remain in the top quartile the following year. The majority of last year's winners are average or below-average performers in the next 12 months.

This is not because the fund managers became worse overnight. It is because outperformance is often cycle-driven: a particular sector or market cap segment had a favourable cycle, and the funds most concentrated there benefited. When the cycle turns, so do the rankings.

Switch to top performer every 2 yearsMultiple tax events, exit loads, consistently buying at cycle peaks, tracking error from frequent transitions

The boring fund wins not because it outperforms in any given year but because the investor holding it does not generate friction costs and does not sell at the wrong time.

The specific cost of category rotation

Switching from one fund category to another creates several compounding costs:

  • Exit load: most equity funds charge 1% on redemptions within 12 months
  • STCG tax: 20% on gains from units held less than a year
  • Reinvestment at peak: new category is bought at elevated valuations
  • Missing the recovery: the abandoned fund often recovers strongly after the switch

An investor who switches categories twice in a decade has paid all four of these costs twice. The investor who held has paid none of them.

What to look at instead of rankings

The question that matters is not "which category returned the most last year?" It is "does this fund consistently do its job relative to its benchmark across multiple market conditions?"

A fund with low tracking error, consistent benchmark-relative performance across rolling 3-year windows, and a stable manager is valuable regardless of whether its category is in fashion. That consistency is what you are paying for.
