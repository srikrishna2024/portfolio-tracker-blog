Most people ask "which fund should I buy?" when the prior question is "what does this fund *do* to my corpus during the specific window before my goal?"

Two funds can both be "equity" and both return 12% over 20 years. But in the 3 years before your goal, one might drop 15% and recover, while the other drops 40% and hasn't recovered yet. Your goal date doesn't care about long-term averages. It only cares about what your corpus is worth on that specific date.

This is the sequence-of-returns problem applied to accumulation, not just retirement. The closer you are to the goal, the more the *path* matters, not the *average*.

---

## Why Volatility Is the Right Variable, Not Returns

The standard way people pick funds is by category and then by historical returns within that category. Both are the wrong primary lens for goal-based matching.

The right variable is **volatility of the corpus in the window before your goal.**

A small cap fund with a 15% long-term CAGR has a standard deviation of roughly 25–30%. In any given year, your actual return can swing between −15% and +45%. Over 20 years, the compounding smooths this out. But in the 3 years before your goal, that 30% standard deviation means a single bad year can wipe out 2–3 years of gains. And unlike a long-horizon investor, you cannot wait for recovery. You need the money.

A large cap index fund with an 11% long-term CAGR has a standard deviation of roughly 15–18%. Smaller swing, smaller recovery problem, smaller sequence risk in the final window.

The return difference between these two funds over the accumulation phase might be real. But as you approach the goal, that extra return advantage shrinks and the volatility disadvantage grows. At roughly 7 years before the goal, the volatility risk begins to outweigh the return benefit for most people.

---

## How Did 7 Years Come From?

It is not a magical number. It is derived from three observable facts about Indian equity markets.

**Fact 1 — The worst-case drawdown and recovery data**

The 2008 crash is the right planning scenario — a genuine economic crisis with slow recovery, not a sentiment crash with a quick bounce.

Nifty 50 fell roughly 55% peak to trough. Recovery to the previous peak took approximately 4 years (by end of 2012). Mid cap and small cap indices took 5–7 years to fully recover, with some individual funds taking longer.

For a large cap or index fund — worst-case recovery time from a severe crash: **4 years.**

**Fact 2 — You need buffer beyond the recovery time**

If the recovery time is 4 years and you have exactly 4 years to your goal, you are betting that no crash happens in year 1. That is not planning — that is hoping. A rational plan builds a buffer of at least 2–3 years beyond the recovery time.

For large cap: 4 years recovery + 2–3 years buffer = **6–7 years** as the threshold where you should begin questioning whether large cap equity is still appropriate.

**Fact 3 — SIP averaging diminishes as corpus grows**

Early in accumulation, your monthly SIP is large relative to your accumulated corpus. A crash at year 3 when your corpus is ₹5L and your monthly SIP is ₹20,000 means new units bought at depressed NAV significantly improve your average cost. The SIP is doing real work.

At year 12 when your corpus is ₹50L and your SIP is still ₹20,000, the new units bought are small relative to the damage done to the existing corpus. The SIP can no longer compensate for the drawdown the way it once could.

This crossover — where accumulated corpus starts to dominate over new SIP flows — typically happens between years 8 and 12. Precisely when you are entering the 7–10 year window before the goal, the equity dampening effect of SIP stops working.

**Putting it together:** 4 years worst-case recovery for large cap + 2–3 year buffer + SIP averaging stopping ≈ **7 years** as the threshold. For mid cap the number is closer to 10. For small cap it is closer to 12.

## The Three Regime Framework

Every goal has three distinct regimes, not one continuous journey. All years below are **years remaining to the goal**, not years since you started investing.

---

### Regime 1 — Accumulation Phase (Beyond 10 Years)

This is where equity does its real work. Time horizon is long enough that:
- A 40% crash has years to recover before it matters
- Volatility works *for* you via SIP — you buy more units at lower NAV during crashes
- The spread of outcomes over 15–20 years narrows considerably

In this regime, higher volatility funds are genuinely appropriate because sequence-of-returns risk is low. The SIP averaging effect actually *rewards* volatility here.

---

### Regime 2 — Transition Phase (5–10 Years)

This is the most misunderstood window. People think "10 years is long — I can still hold small cap." But this is where the risk shifts character.

In this window:
- A major crash (2008-scale, −50%) needs 4–5 years to recover. You may not have that buffer
- SIP averaging still helps but the corpus is now large enough that NAV crashes hurt more in absolute terms than new units help
- The *size* of the existing corpus starts to dominate over new contributions

The question is no longer "can I survive volatility?" — it is "can my accumulated corpus survive volatility?" These are different questions. A small corpus in year 2 dropping 40% recovers easily via SIP. A large corpus in year 7 dropping 40% may not recover before year 10.

In this regime, shift toward large cap and flexi cap. Not because mid cap is bad — but because its volatility is now working against you rather than for you.

---

### Regime 3 — Protection Phase (Under 5 Years)

Zero equity. Not because equity always falls in the short term — sometimes it doesn't. But because the risk you cannot afford is not "equity might underperform" — it is "equity might be down 30% when I need the money and I have no time to wait."

This is not a return optimisation decision. It is a goal certainty decision. The corpus is now worth more protected than grown.

---

## The Double Error

Choosing a high-volatility fund and planning with high-return assumptions from it compounds two errors.

**Error 1 — Planning with the optimistic end of a wide spread.** A small cap fund has a 15-year return spread that might range from 8% to 22% depending on start and end date. If you plan with 15% assumed return and land at 9%, your corpus gap is enormous. If you had planned with 10% and landed at 9%, the gap is manageable.

**Error 2 — Volatility itself reduces your effective compound return.** A fund that goes −40%, +50%, +12% delivers a lower compound return than one that goes +5%, +12%, +5% even if their simple averages are similar. Volatility is a drag on compounding. The more volatile the fund, the larger this drag — and planning with the long-term average ignores this drag entirely.

Both errors tilt your plan toward optimism. Together they mean the corpus you arrive at the goal date with is systematically likely to be lower than your projection.

## The Recovery Time Framework

The most useful lens for timeline matching is not the SEBI category label — it is the **maximum drawdown and typical recovery period** for each fund type.

| Category | Worst drawdown | Recovery time | Minimum timeline needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Debt / Gilt | Near zero | Immediate | Any timeline |
| Large cap / Index | 40–45% | 3–4 years | 6–7 years minimum |
| Flexi cap (large-biased) | 40–50% | 4–5 years | 7–8 years minimum |
| Mid cap | 55–65% | 5–7 years | 9–10 years minimum |
| Small cap | 65–75% | 7–9 years | 11–12 years minimum |

The decision question to ask before mapping any fund to a goal:

> **If this fund has its worst-case drawdown today, would the corpus recover to my required level by the goal date?**

If yes — the fund is timeline-appropriate. If no — it is not, regardless of return potential.

## Which Fund for Which Timeline

### Under 5 Years — Zero Equity

No equity fund of any category. Not large cap, not balanced advantage, not aggressive hybrid.

The worst-case drawdown for the least volatile equity category — large cap index — is 35–45% with a recovery time of 4 years. If your goal is 4 years away and this plays out on day 1, you reach the goal date still underwater or barely recovered.

The expected return advantage of equity over debt for a 4-year window — roughly 3–4% annually — does not compensate for the asymmetry. In the best case, equity gives you 3–4% more per year. In the bad case, it gives you a 30–40% corpus reduction with no recovery time.

There is also a near-certain behavioural cost. An investor watching their corpus fall 25% with 3 years to a non-negotiable goal — child's college fees, property purchase — will almost certainly sell at the bottom. Holding equity this close to a hard deadline is not just financially risky. It is behaviourally near-impossible.

**Use instead:** Target maturity debt funds matched to the goal date, or high-quality short duration debt funds.

---

### 5–10 Years — Up to 25% Equity, Large Cap or Large-Biased Flexi Cap Only

No mid cap. No small cap. No thematic funds.

Mid cap funds have a worst-case drawdown of 55–65% and a recovery time of 5–7 years. At 8 years to goal, a 2008-scale crash means mid cap may not recover in time. You would arrive at the goal with your equity component still depressed.

Large cap has a worst-case drawdown of 35–45% and a recovery time of 4 years. At 8 years to goal: 4 years recovery + 4 years remaining. Tight, but within rational planning.

**The 25% maximum is a ceiling for the 10-year end of this band, not a target throughout.** A sensible glide path within this band:
- 10 years to goal: 25% equity (large cap only)
- 8 years to goal: 20% equity
- 6 years to goal: 10–15% equity
- 5 years to goal: 0–10% equity, actively shifting to zero

**On flexi cap:** A flexi cap fund with a 60% large-cap allocation behaves like a large cap fund in a crash. One with 40% small-cap allocation does not. Check the actual portfolio — not just the category label. If the fund manager has a strong mid/small tilt, it is inappropriate for this timeline.

**On balanced advantage funds:** They adjust equity dynamically based on the fund manager's valuation model — not your goal date. The fund does not know you have 7 years left. A separately held debt fund matched to your goal date and a large cap equity fund gives you more control and transparency than a BAF in this window.

---

### Beyond 10 Years — Up to 50–60% Equity, All Categories Appropriate

This is the only window where mid cap and small cap have a legitimate role.

Beyond 10 years, even the worst-case recovery time for small cap (7–8 years) fits within the available window with buffer remaining. A 2008-scale crash hitting when you have 15 years to go means 7–8 years of recovery still leaves 7–8 years of growth before the goal.

The SIP averaging effect is also genuinely powerful here — you are in early-to-middle accumulation, corpus is small relative to future contributions, and buying at depressed NAVs meaningfully improves average cost.

**Which equity for which part of the 10+ year window:**

**10–12 years to goal:** Large cap 60%, mid cap 40% within the equity allocation. Small cap is borderline — its worst-case recovery time of 7–8 years consumes almost the entire buffer at a 10-year horizon. If you include small cap, limit it to 10–15% of total equity and have a clear plan to migrate out by year 5.

**12–15 years to goal:** Large cap 50%, mid cap 30–40%, small cap 10–20% within equity. The additional buffer makes small cap more comfortable. SIP averaging is working at full power.

**Beyond 15 years:** All categories are timeline-appropriate. The binding constraint shifts from timeline to temperament — someone who cannot emotionally hold through a 60% drawdown should hold less small cap regardless of the mathematical sufficiency of the time horizon. Risk appetite, unknowable until you experience a real crash, becomes the binding constraint.

## The Two Dials

Think of approaching a goal as two dials turning simultaneously:
- **Dial 1:** Equity percentage decreasing (the standard advice everyone gives)
- **Dial 2:** Equity *type* becoming less volatile (the advice almost nobody gives)

Both dials should be turning. Someone who reduces equity from 60% to 30% as they approach the goal but keeps the 30% in small cap has addressed Dial 1 but not Dial 2. Their remaining equity is now more concentrated in high-volatility assets — which is worse per unit of equity than holding large cap at 60%.

The Freefincal framework addresses Dial 1 explicitly. Dial 2 is the implicit layer that most goal-based frameworks leave to the investor's judgment — and most investors get it wrong by defaulting to "I'll just keep my existing funds."

---

## The Migration Schedule

The reason this matters practically is that most people build their portfolio in their 30s — when all goals are 15–20 years away and high-volatility equity is genuinely appropriate. They then *keep the same funds* as goals approach. The portfolio that was right at year 1 becomes wrong at year 12 not because the funds changed but because the investor's relationship to time changed.

The migration path — from small cap to mid cap to large cap to debt — as a goal approaches is not about market calls. It is about mechanically adjusting which equity you hold as the recovery-time buffer shrinks.

The schedule is simple: **when remaining time equals the typical recovery time for your fund category, begin the migration to the next lower-volatility category.** Not because the fund is bad. Because the window for it to do its job and recover from mistakes has closed.

Done on a schedule — not on a market view — it removes both the behavioural error of panic-selling and the planning error of holding the wrong volatility at the wrong time.
